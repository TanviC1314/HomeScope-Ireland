# Housing Affordability Pressure Index

_Generated by `python -m src.indicators.affordability_report` from the processed data - every number below was computed, not typed in.
Code: `src/indicators/affordability_index.py`; dashboard page: **Affordability Index**; tests: `tests/test_affordability_index.py`._

## 1. What it is (and is not)

A transparent, county-level ranking of how much affordability pressure each of the 26 counties faces *relative to the other counties*, combining
rental cost, rent rises, property-price growth and income. **A higher score means less affordable than other counties - it is not an absolute
affordability threshold and not a forecast.** The weights are a documented judgement, not estimated from data, and the sensitivity analysis
(section 4) shows how much the ranking depends on them.

## 2. Formula

1. Compute four components per county (raw values in section 3).
2. Standardise each component across the 26 counties to 0-100 (min-max: 0 = lowest-pressure county, 100 = highest).
3. `Index = sum(weight_i x standardised_i)` with weights summing to 1. Rank 1 = highest pressure (least affordable). Tiers are quartiles of the index.

| Component | Weight | Lens | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rent burden (median rent as % of tenant disposable income) | 35% | Rent vs income (measured) | CSO TRS38 (RTB tenancies), 2024; local-authority medians weighted by dwelling stock |
| Annual rent as % of disposable income per person | 25% | Rent level vs income (relative) | RTB average rent (CSO RIQ02), latest quarter / CSO RAA02 income per person |
| Rent rise since base period, as % of annual income per person | 20% | Rent dynamics vs income | CSO RIQ02 (RTB) change in average monthly rent x 12 / CSO RAA02 income per person |
| Regional house-price growth since base period | 20% | Property prices | CSO HPM09 residential property price index (houses), NUTS3 region of the county |

Component definitions:

* **Rent burden** - median rent as % of tenant disposable income, 2024 (CSO TRS38 by local authority, combined to counties using dwelling stock as weights).
  The only directly measured rent-to-income share, hence the largest weight.
* **Rent vs income per person** - 12 x average monthly rent (RTB, Q4 2025) / disposable income per person (CSO RAA02, latest year) x 100. Adds whole-county rent
  levels; per-person income overstates the burden, so only the *relative* position is used.
* **Rent rise vs income** - 100 x 12 x (rent Q4 2025 - rent Q4 2019) / disposable income per person. The euro rise (not % growth) is used because % growth is
  mechanically larger where rents started low: in a first version using % growth, the level and growth components were strongly negatively correlated across counties
  and cancelled each other, so the ranking depended almost entirely on which lens was weighted.
* **Regional price growth** - % change in the CSO house-price index (HPM09, houses) between Dec 2019 and Sep 2026 for the county's NUTS3 region (Dublin uses the Dublin
  series; the CSO's "Midland" is the project's "Midlands"). **The CSO publishes no euro price levels and no county series**, so a county price-to-income ratio cannot be
  built honestly; counties in a region share one value.

Correlation of the components across the 26 counties (regional price growth is negatively related to the level measures: regions that were already expensive in 2019 grew less since):

## 4. Sensitivity analysis

* Minimum Spearman rank correlation with the baseline: **0.25** across six named weighting scenarios, **0.37** when one
  component is dropped, **0.92** across three standardisation methods.
* Median 5th-95th percentile rank range: **5** places for weights near the baseline, **15** for arbitrary weights.
* Baseline top five: Dublin, Galway, Limerick, Meath, Wicklow. Of these, in the top 8 in *every* named scenario: **Galway, Limerick, Wicklow**.
* Largest rank change of any county between the baseline and a named scenario: **23** places.

**Interpretation.** The standardisation method barely matters; the *weights* matter a lot. Weighting the rent burden and rent-to-income measures heavily puts the Dublin commuter belt
and Limerick/Galway at the top, while weighting price growth heavily lifts counties whose prices rose fastest since 2019 (mostly regions that started cheaper) and pushes Dublin down.
The ranking is best read in tiers rather than as a precise order: counties one or two places apart are not meaningfully different.

## 5. Limitations

* Relative ranking only: no absolute threshold, not a forecast, not a measure of housing quality, supply or need.
* No county property prices exist: price pressure enters as regional growth (8 distinct values for 26 counties) and says nothing about price levels.
* Per-person income understates household income; the rent-vs-income components overstate levels (relative position only).
* Weights are a judgement. The default favours the measured tenant rent burden; other defensible weightings give different orders (section 4).
* Rent data are RTB new-tenancy averages and exclude owner-occupiers; mortgage affordability (Central Bank data) is not included.
* The index is not validated against any external benchmark; it organises published statistics transparently.
